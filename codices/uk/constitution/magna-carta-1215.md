# Magna Carta

The Great Charter is the root inscription of the English constitution: the first
acknowledgement in writing that the Crown itself is under the law.

## What it changed

The 1215 Charter forced King John to concede that royal power had limits enforceable by
his subjects: no taxation without common counsel, no sale or denial of justice, no
imprisonment of a free man except by lawful judgment of his equals or the law of the land.

## The surviving text

Only three chapters of the 1297 statute remain in force in England and Wales:

- **Chapter 1** — the English Church shall be free.
- **Chapter 9** — the City of London shall retain its ancient liberties.
- **Chapter 29** — *"No freeman shall be taken or imprisoned … but by lawful judgment of
  his peers, or by the law of the land. We will sell to no man, we will not deny or defer
  to any man either justice or right."*

Chapter 29 (clauses 39 and 40 of the 1215 Charter) is the ancestor of due process
guarantees worldwide, including the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments to the US
Constitution.

## Chamber record

Magna Carta predates Parliament. It was extracted at sword-point, annulled by a pope,
reissued three times, and finally confirmed as statute by Edward I in 1297 in exchange
for taxation — a reminder that constitutional history is a negotiation, not a ceremony.
