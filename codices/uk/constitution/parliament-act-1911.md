# Parliament Act 1911

The statute in which the elected chamber established its supremacy over the hereditary
one — passed under threat of flooding the House of Lords with five hundred new peers.

## What it changed

- **Money bills** — a bill certified by the Speaker as a money bill becomes law one
  month after leaving the Commons, with or without the Lords' agreement.
- **Other public bills** — the Lords' veto is replaced by a delaying power: a bill
  passed by the Commons in three successive sessions over two years (reduced to two
  sessions over one year in 1949) becomes law without the Lords' consent.
- **Five-year parliaments** — the maximum life of a Parliament is cut from seven years
  (Septennial Act 1715) to five, where it remains today.
- The preamble promises to replace the Lords with a chamber "constituted on a popular
  instead of hereditary basis" — a motion that has remained open for over a century.

## Chamber record

The Act is the canonical example of a chamber rewriting the rules of the chambers. It
took two general elections, a secret royal guarantee to create peers, and a final Lords
division of 131–114 (10 August 1911) before Royal Assent on 18 August 1911. The Acts
have since been used to enact, among others, the War Crimes Act 1991, the European
Parliamentary Elections Act 1999, the Sexual Offences (Amendment) Act 2000 and the
Hunting Act 2004.
