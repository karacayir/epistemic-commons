# Habeas Corpus Act 1679

The Act that turned an ancient common-law writ into an enforceable machine against
arbitrary imprisonment.

## What it changed

The writ of *habeas corpus* — "produce the body" — existed long before 1679, but kings
and gaolers had learned to evade it: prisoners were moved between prisons, shipped to
islands beyond the writ's reach, or simply held while judges delayed. The Act closed the
loopholes with bureaucratic precision:

- Gaolers must produce the prisoner and the true cause of detention within strict time
  limits, on pain of heavy fines.
- No prisoner may be moved from prison to prison to evade the writ.
- No subject may be imprisoned "beyond the seas" — outside the reach of English courts.
- A prisoner released by habeas corpus may not be re-committed for the same offence.

## Chamber record

The Act was passed in the fevered spring of 1679, amid the Exclusion Crisis. Bishop
Gilbert Burnet's contemporary account records that it passed the Lords only because a
teller jokingly counted one enormously fat peer as ten votes — and the miscount was never
challenged. Whatever the truth of the story, the Act received Royal Assent on
27 May 1679, hours before Parliament was prorogued, and has guarded the liberty of the
subject ever since.
