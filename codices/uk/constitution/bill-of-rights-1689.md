# Bill of Rights 1689

The contract of the Glorious Revolution: Parliament offers the Crown, and the Crown
accepts limits. It is the closest thing England has to a founding document of
parliamentary sovereignty.

## What it changed

The Act declared "ancient rights and liberties" — in reality, a new constitutional
settlement:

- **No royal law-making** — suspending or dispensing with laws by royal authority
  without consent of Parliament is illegal.
- **No taxation without Parliament** — levying money for the Crown without
  parliamentary grant is illegal.
- **No standing army in peacetime** without the consent of Parliament.
- **Free elections** — election of Members of Parliament ought to be free.
- **Article 9: parliamentary privilege** — "the freedome of speech and debates or
  proceedings in Parlyament ought not to be impeached or questioned in any court or
  place out of Parlyament." Still litigated in the Supreme Court today.
- **No excessive bail, no cruel and unusual punishments** — language inherited verbatim
  by the Eighth Amendment to the US Constitution a century later.
- The right of subjects to **petition the King** — the ancestor of the petition systems
  this platform generalises.

## Chamber record

The Bill began as the Declaration of Right, presented to William and Mary on
13 February 1689 as the condition of the Crown. Parliament then enacted the Declaration
as statute, with Royal Assent on 16 December 1689. Its succession clauses were amended
as recently as 2013, when the Succession to the Crown Act ended male-preference
primogeniture — a live document after 336 years.
