# Human Rights Act 1998

"Rights brought home": the Act that made the European Convention on Human Rights
enforceable in British courts, sixty years after British lawyers helped draft it.

## What it changed

Before 2000, a person whose Convention rights were violated had to exhaust domestic
remedies and then take the United Kingdom to court in Strasbourg — a process of years.
The Act rewired this:

- **Section 3** — courts must read all legislation, past and future, compatibly with
  Convention rights "so far as it is possible to do so".
- **Section 4** — where a statute cannot be read compatibly, higher courts may issue a
  **declaration of incompatibility**. The statute stands — parliamentary sovereignty is
  preserved — but the incompatibility becomes a public, permanent fact that Parliament
  is invited to fix.
- **Section 6** — it is unlawful for any public authority (including courts) to act
  incompatibly with Convention rights.
- **Section 19** — every minister introducing a Bill must publish a statement on its
  compatibility with Convention rights: a compatibility check on every new motion.

## Chamber record

Introduced in the Lords by Lord Chancellor Lord Irvine of Lairg on 23 October 1997,
carried in the Commons by Home Secretary Jack Straw, Royal Assent 9 November 1998,
main commencement 2 October 2000. The Act has survived two decades of proposals to
replace it, most recently the Bill of Rights Bill, withdrawn in June 2023. In this
platform's terms: several motions to repeal have been opened; none has passed a single
chamber.
