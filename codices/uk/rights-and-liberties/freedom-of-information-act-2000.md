# Freedom of Information Act 2000

The statute that reversed the default: information held by the state belongs to the
public, and secrecy is the exception that must be justified.

## What it changed

- **A general right of access** — any person, anywhere in the world, may request
  information from a UK public authority and must normally receive it within 20 working
  days. No reason need be given; motives are irrelevant.
- **Duty to confirm or deny** — authorities must generally say whether they hold the
  information at all.
- **Exemptions with a public-interest test** — most exemptions (policy formulation,
  commercial interests, legal privilege) apply only where the public interest in
  secrecy outweighs the public interest in disclosure.
- **The Information Commissioner** — independent enforcement, with appeal to a
  tribunal, so refusals are themselves reviewable public records.

## Chamber record

Promised in the 1997 Labour manifesto, shaped by David Clark's white paper *Your Right
to Know*, introduced by Jack Straw, and given Royal Assent on 30 November 2000 — then
held back for four years, coming fully into force on 1 January 2005. Its most famous
consequence was the MPs' expenses affair of 2009, which began as a set of FOI requests
by journalists. Tony Blair later called the Act his greatest regret in office; this
codex records that assessment as the Act's highest commendation.
