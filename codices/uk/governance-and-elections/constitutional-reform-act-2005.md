# Constitutional Reform Act 2005

The Act that separated the powers Britain had genially left mixed for centuries: the
judge who was also a minister who was also the speaker of a legislative chamber.

## What it changed

- **The Supreme Court** — a Supreme Court of the United Kingdom, replacing the
  Appellate Committee of the House of Lords. The highest judges left the legislature;
  the Court opened on 1 October 2009 across Parliament Square.
- **The Lord Chancellor, reformed** — the ancient office survived (the Lords refused
  its abolition) but lost its judicial functions: no longer head of the judiciary, no
  longer speaker of the Lords, no longer required to be a lawyer or a peer. The Lord
  Chief Justice became head of the judiciary of England and Wales.
- **Judicial independence, in statute** — section 3 places all ministers under a
  statutory duty to uphold the independence of the judiciary.
- **The Judicial Appointments Commission** — judges selected by independent
  commission on merit, ending appointment by ministerial tap on the shoulder.

## Chamber record

Announced with startling casualness in a June 2003 reshuffle, the reform was then
subjected to two years of genuine scrutiny — including a rare special select committee
in the Lords — before Royal Assent on 24 March 2005. The episode illustrates both
failure modes this platform exists to prevent (constitutional change by surprise) and
the corrective value of slow, visible chambers.
